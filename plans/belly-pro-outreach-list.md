# Belly for Professionals: outreach targets

Built 2026-09-21. Companion to `belly-for-professionals.md`. Target: 10
discovery calls this week.

Scope: independent solo practitioners. Clinics, multi-practitioner teams and
franchise-style groups are explicitly deprioritized, with the reasoning in
section 1.

Every name below came from a public web search result and runs a public practice
website. None are invented. Contact details are not listed here on purpose. Pull
them from each site's own contact or booking page at the time of sending, so
nothing here goes stale or wrong.

## 1. Why independents, not clinics

Right instinct, and it holds for four separate reasons.

Buying behaviour. A solo practitioner pays with her own credit card and decides
in one call. A clinic has an office manager, an existing stack and a budget
cycle. For a product that does not exist yet, a one-call decision is the only
kind worth chasing this week.

Product fit. A clinic needs charting, scheduling, multi-practitioner permissions
and often billing. That is Practice Better's product, and
`belly-for-professionals.md` says do not build it. A solo coach needs exactly
what Belly already has: build a plan, send it, have the client actually use it.

Signal quality. A clinic answer is "we use whatever we bought three years ago."
A solo answer is "I spend Sunday night building plans in a Google Doc and I hate
it." Only the second one is data.

Pain intensity. A solo coach personally does the meal planning labour. A clinic
owner has delegated it. The person doing the work is the person who feels the
problem.

Tradeoff to accept: solo coaches churn hard, because their businesses fail. That
is in the plan's risk list and is the price of this segment.

## 2. How to find hundreds, and how to screen them

### Screening heuristic

The directories return clinics and solos mixed together. Tell them apart from
the listing alone:

Solo signals. One name and one face on the homepage. First person voice, "I work
with," "my approach." A "Work with me" nav item. Packages sold in 3 or 6 month
blocks. A Calendly or Acuity booking link rather than a phone number. Instagram
in the nav. Practice name is a person's name, or a two-word phrase with no
"Clinic," "Centre," "Group," "Health" or "Associates" in it.

Clinic signals. A "Our team" or "Meet the practitioners" page. Multiple
locations. "Direct billing" or "insurance accepted" above the fold. A phone
number as the primary call to action. A reception email. Anything with Centre,
Clinic, Institute, Group, Associates or Medicine in the name.

Ambiguous but worth a call. A solo practitioner with one or two contractors.
Still decides alone.

### Instagram, the channel that actually matches

Solo coaches in these three cities market on Instagram, not on Google. A DM
outperforms a cold email for this group by a wide margin.

Tags and combinations to search: nutritioncoach plus toronto,
holisticnutritionist plus toronto, hormonehealth plus vancouver, cyclesyncing,
pcoscoach, nutritionnistemontreal, rhncoach.

Target band: 2,000 to 20,000 followers with a booking link in bio. Below 2k they
have no client book yet and nothing to tell you. Above 50k they run a course or
supplement business, have staff, and will not take the call. The band is the
segment.

## 3.5 Cycle syncing specialists, the wedge list

This is the section that matters. `belly-for-professionals.md` section 7 argues
the cycle method is what keeps the defensibility argument alive through the move
to B2B, and that women's health coaching is a real niche with close to zero
purpose-built tooling. These are the people who would prove or kill that.

Treat this list as the primary target set and the general list in section 3 as
the control group. If cycle specialists react measurably harder than general
nutrition coaches, the wedge is real. If they react the same, Belly Pro is a
generic meal planning tool and should be priced and positioned as one.

### Geography matters less here

Cycle syncing coaching is almost entirely virtual, because the client base is
too thin in any one city to fill a book locally. Several of the strongest
specialists below serve clients nationally or internationally from a home base.
That is good news: the wedge has a far larger addressable pool than the
three-city constraint implies, and a coach in Kelowna is as reachable as one in
Toronto.

Keep the three cities as the starting point for the cofounder's warm intros,
then widen to Canada-wide and then North America for this niche specifically.

### Positioning caution

Some coaches in this niche make strong hormonal and clinical claims.
`belly-for-professionals.md` section 7 and the original strategy doc both hold
the line that Belly frames this experientially, as responding to how you feel
across your cycle, with no clinical or hormone-balancing claims.

Two consequences for these calls. Do not adopt a prospect's claim language just
because she uses it, because whatever gets said on a sales call ends up in the
marketing copy. And listen for whether a coach needs the software itself to make
claims, because a coach who wants Belly to tell her client that a food balances
a hormone is a coach we cannot serve without taking on her exposure.

## 4. Call order

Tier 1, cycle specialists in the three cities. Nutrition Moderation, Get Well To
Do, Karine Mousseau, Fannie Beaulieu, Health Coach Mama, Trista Chan. This tier
is the experiment. Everything else is the control.

Tier 2, cycle specialists outside the three cities, virtual. Composed Nutrition,
Maddy Pollack, The Women's Dietitian, plus whoever the Institute for Menstrual
Health directory turns up. Run these only after tier 1, so the warm intros go
first.

Tier 3, general meal planning is visibly the service they sell. Diet Redefined,
Kim Arrey, The Nourished Way, Sarah Goldstein, Sarah Berneche. The control
group.

Tier 4, personal-brand and fitness crossover, currently paying the Trainerize
nutrition add-on tax. Train Like Rob, Unlocked, SCN2xL, Iron Lab.

Tier 5, French-first Montreal generalists. Francine Allard, Manutritionniste,
Nutrition à la Carte. Run to test whether French is a buying reason.

### One extra question for tier 1 and 2

Added to the six in section 5, ask only the cycle specialists:

7. When a client is in a different phase this week, what actually changes in the
   food you give her, and how do you produce that today?

The answer tells us whether phase-aware planning is a real workflow with real
labour attached, or a framing they talk about and do not operationalise. That
single answer decides whether Belly builds cycle-aware planning or just markets
to women.

## 5. What to ask

Discovery, not a pitch. Nothing is built, so do not sell.

1. Walk me through building a week of meals for one client. How long does it
   take?
2. What software are you in right now, and what do you pay across all of it?
3. What is the worst part of the current tool?
4. When a client wants a recipe swapped, or the plan scaled for a partner, what
   happens?
5. How do clients receive the plan, and do they actually use it?
6. If a tool did X, what would you pay and would you pay today?

Record objections verbatim. Per the plan, the deliverable from this week is a
pre-sell conversion rate, not a feature list.

## 6. Honest limits

Roughly twenty-two verified names is a starting set, not a census. Comprehensive
lives in the directories in section 2, screened with the heuristic at the top of
it. Practice size was inferred from public site copy and search snippets, not
confirmed, so verify solo status on the site before each call rather than
trusting the Read column.

Contact details were deliberately not collected or listed. Pull them fresh from
each practice's own contact page when sending, and respect CASL: Canadian
anti-spam law governs commercial electronic messages. Business-to-business
outreach to a publicly published business address about that business is on
solid ground, but include a real identity and a working unsubscribe path in
every email. Instagram DMs fall outside CASL but inside Meta's own limits, so
keep the volume human.
