# Product Principles

The quality bar. What makes a great product. What kills one.

---

## What makes a great product

A great product solves a real problem so well that users feel its absence when it is gone. The best ones combine three things:

1. **Sharp focus.** Does one thing exceptionally rather than many things adequately. Saying no is the hardest part.
2. **Taste in defaults.** Opinionated where it matters, flexible where it does not. Users should not have to think about decisions the product can make for them.
3. **Respect for the user's time.** Fast, reliable, predictable. Every friction point is a small betrayal.

Underneath all of it: a clear point of view about who it is for and what it refuses to be.

## The pitfalls that prevent products from being great

1. **Trying to please everyone.** Every added persona dilutes the core experience. "Enterprise asked for it" is how good products die.
2. **Feature accretion over depth.** Shipping breadth instead of polishing what is already there. Each feature taxes every future decision.
3. **Building for the demo, not the daily use.** Looks great in a screenshot, painful on day 30. The 100th session matters more than the first.
4. **Confusing motion with progress.** Velocity metrics, sprint counts, ship cadence — none of it matters if users are not getting more value.
5. **Ignoring the boring middle.** Onboarding and launch get attention; the long flat middle of the user journey gets neglected and that is where churn happens.
6. **Listening to users literally.** They describe symptoms, not causes. Building exactly what they ask for usually misses what they need.
7. **No taste at the top.** When leadership cannot say "that is not us," the product becomes a committee artifact.
8. **Treating tech debt as optional.** Slowness compounds silently until every change feels expensive and risky.

The meta-pitfall: **optimizing for what is measurable instead of what matters.**

## How we decide what to build

Run every proposed feature, change, or initiative through these:

1. **Would we use this every day?** If not, do not ship it.
2. **Does this make the product more itself, or more like everyone else?** Drift toward sameness is the enemy.
3. **Are we adding because users need it, or because we are afraid to say no?** Fear-driven features are how we become the thing we are replacing.
4. **Is this something only we would build?** If a competent team at the incumbent could ship the same thing, we are not far enough out on the edge.
5. **What does this cost us — not to build, but to maintain forever?** Every feature is a permanent tax.

## The quality bar

- **Would-be-upset test.** Users would be genuinely upset if we shut this down. Not "satisfied." Not "engaged." Upset.
- **Show-a-friend test.** Would we proudly show this to a friend whose taste we respect? If not, do not ship it.
- **Day-30 test.** Will this still feel right on the user's 30th session, not just their first?
- **The 80/20 we will not accept.** Final 20% of polish is what separates loved from tolerated. We do that work.

## Listening to users

- Talk to users constantly. 5+ conversations a week, minimum.
- Hear the *symptom* in what they say. Find the *cause* yourself.
- Watch what they do, not what they say they do.
- Take feature requests as signals about pain, not specs.
- The loudest user is rarely the most representative. Triangulate.
- Say no often, gracefully, with reasoning. Users respect a clear point of view.

## Shipping

- Ship small, ship often. A weekly visible improvement compounds.
- Polish before scope. Better to ship one thing well than three things half-done.
- Public changelog. Even tiny things. Cadence is brand.
- Postmortems for everything that broke. Internally honest, externally clear.
- Never ship to win a deal we should not have been in.

## What we refuse, in product terms

- **Generic AI features** ("we added AI!") that any incumbent will copy in a sprint. We use AI to make the product more itself, not to chase a buzzword.
- **Settings as an answer to disagreement.** If we cannot decide, we have not understood the problem yet. Settings are an admission of failure when the question was a design choice.
- **Dark patterns.** Ever. Even for retention. Even if they "work."
- **Lock-in for its own sake.** We earn loyalty. Users can leave. Most will not.
- **Roadmap by loudest user.** Listen to all, decide alone.

## The compounding loop

Great product → users tell friends → audience grows → talk to more users → product gets sharper → repeat.

Everything that breaks this loop — feature bloat, distraction, optimizing for wrong metrics, hiring the wrong person — is the enemy.

Protect the loop. Run the loop. Trust the loop.
